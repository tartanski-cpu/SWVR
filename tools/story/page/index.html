A sea game, built by talking

Workboat World is a browser VR game: chunky workboats you operate from inside, with physical controls, on a sea with real hydrostatics. It was built in plain English by a naval architect who doesn't write code, working with Claude Code, in sixteen days: the first scaffold on 12 October 2026, v1.0 on about the fifth day, this deck on the 28th. This is the story of how it went.

What it is

Workboats you drive from the inside

Played on a Meta Quest 3 at 90 Hz, or on a desktop. Solo, or peer-to-peer with friends in their own boats. No install: a web address.

  • Career: start in a cheap gillnetter, take cargo runs, rescues, tows, fires, potting and fishing; earn, repair, buy a better boat at the yard.
  • Voyage: five real coastlines from open map data, at full scale: Oban, Loch Sween, the Firth of Clyde, Syros, Turku and the Archipelago Sea.
  • Shipyard: design your own boat, hull to paint; the game's physics sea-trials her as you go; her design is a 17-character code.
  • The boats: a 12 m displacement workboat to begin with; now a fleet from a 6 m RIB to a 25 m harbour tug with azimuth drives and a walk-in engine room.

How it was made

Briefs in plain words, milestones, tests in a headset

  • The owner writes a brief the way they'd brief a yard: what the boat should do and feel like, in naval-architecture terms where that's natural. Photos and sketches when words won't do.
  • Claude Code plans milestones and stops after each one: "done, awaiting owner test". The owner tests in VR on the Quest and on a desktop, then writes feedback. Three rounds on the bridge controls alone.
  • Standing rules are written down and enforced: keep it cheap on the Quest (watch the draw calls), no new physics bodies or particle systems without asking, flag unwise requests instead of building them, verify before reporting.
  • The sea-trials lab is the acceptance test: every physics change prints speed, turning circle, stopping distance, GM three ways. Later, golden runs: twelve recorded sessions every refactor must reproduce to a millionth.
  • Everything is logged. An 8,200-line progress file says what was built, why, what was measured and what wasn't checked. This deck is distilled from it.
  • Since 23 Oct, five chats work side by side, one per area of the code, each with its own files, settings, log and preview address.

From the first scaffold to today: sixteen days

Milestone 2 · live

The sea the hull can feel

Eight Gerstner wave components live in the world state, so the host can change the weather and every player sees the same sea. The GPU sums them for the picture: one draw call, 1 m cells near you growing 8 % a cell to a 3 km horizon.

The trick is on the CPU: Gerstner waves move the water sideways as well as up, so asking "how high is the water here?" needs the displacement inverted. surfaceHeight(x, z) does it by fixed-point iteration, six passes, under a centimetre in a storm. The yellow probes are that function. They sit on the drawn surface because both do the same sums, and the render runs exactly one tick behind the simulation so they can never drift apart.

Lines plans · live

A hull from a handful of curves

Before modular boats, a study: could the game loft hulls the way a naval architect sketches a type? A HullDesign is a few curves along the length, sheer, half-breadth, keel profile, section shape, each a handful of control points joined by a monotone cubic. loft() turns them into stations; buildHullMesh() panels them; hydrostatics() integrates displacement, LCB, LCF, the form coefficients and KM at the design waterline, catamarans included.

Fifteen original types, from a 7 m push tug to a 25 m ASD harbour tug. The shortlist became the game's boats; the harbour tug's lines went straight into the Shipyard as a one-off at 494 t and Cb 0.59.

Milestone 3 · live

Strip theory, every tick

No physics engine: a hand-written rigid body with semi-implicit Euler, body-axis angular velocity with the gyroscopic term, and added mass per axis. Buoyancy is strip theory on the real hull: each station is a polygon, clipped at the local wave surface every tick, pushing up with ρ·g·A at its immersed centroid. The blue outlines are those stations; the yellow arrows the forces.

That gives displacement, trim and the large-angle GZ curve for free, with deck-edge immersion, and the same stations loft the hull you see. Drop her from 4 m, heel her 20°, or turn the sea up and watch her roll. The owner's first drop test found a bow-up rebound; it was missing water-entry energy loss, and the fix is a quadratic water-entry drag.

The first game

Workboat World v1.0

One 12 m displacement workboat, a hand-built archipelago, and a career. Tagged by the owner as the restore point before the next big step.

  • A whole ship's electrics as plain state: two 100 Ah batteries, a selector, breakers, a starter drawing 180 A that cranks slowly on a weak battery, glow plugs, a laminated start-up card. The game starts cold.
  • Weather with a director: five presets that only make allowed transitions, a sea that builds over 90 s and decays over 160 s, rain as 1,400 shader-driven line segments, wipers computed from their clock, lightning, a shipping-forecast read-out on the VHF.
  • A paper chart you draw on with a real pencil, marks synced as small ops; AI traffic with fog signals; an anchor with chain tension; docking lines as damped springs.
  • Rescues, tows, fires, cargo, salvage, trawling: each flagged against the standing rules first (a towline as one spring-damper, not a rope; a fire jet as one instanced mesh along its arc, not a particle system).
  • Sound synthesised, not sampled: an AudioWorklet fires each cylinder of a six at rpm/20 Hz; later matched to a real recording within 3 dB from 40 Hz to 3 kHz.
  • Peer-to-peer multiplayer with a host-authoritative world, added without touching the physics, because the world was plain JSON driven by intents from day one.

After v1.0 · live

A boat is a hull plus a recipe

The next step was to make every boat out of modules, so the Shipyard could build any of them. A module has two halves: a simulation half (plain functions over plain JSON, host only) and a view half (meshes and controls, every player). Modules never import each other; they only read and write named keys on a bus: throttle.demand, engine.rpm. A recipe says which module goes in which socket, with interlocks like hatch.engine.open == false.

The refactor took seven milestones and nothing was allowed to look or handle differently. Twelve golden runs had to reproduce to nine significant figures; screenshots had to be pixel-identical; draw calls could only fall. They did: 67 → 56 at the helm, 120 → 95 on the aft deck, because every toggle bat became one instanced mesh.

The Shipyard · live

Design a boat; the physics sea-trials her

The Shipyard is a page where you pick a hull type and move sliders. Every move runs the game's builder: it scales the hull's lines, weighs her part by part (plating at 35 kg/m² in aluminium or 85 in steel, houses, machinery, gear), then raises or lowers her lines until she displaces exactly that weight. The engine goes on her shaft line where she balances; the wheelhouse and console are fit-checked; a hatch is cut over the engine.

Then the game's own sea trials run on her in a background worker: a halving search over sixteen gearbox ratios so flat out settles at 93 % of rated rpm, then speed, range, turning circle, stopping and bollard pull. Her design is a short code (Crockford base-32 with a check character) you can type in VR, or a shared link; two people can design together over the game's own peer link, with no server.

Eating our own dog food · live

The game's boats are Shipyard designs

Once the Shipyard worked, the owner designed the game's own fleet in it: a fast landing craft, a gillnetter, a pilot boat on pod drives, a small tug, the beginner boat, a dive support vessel. Each is kept as her design file and built by the same builder when the game loads, so a bug fixed in the yard is fixed in the fleet.

Hull types ride as their kind: the landing craft planes and banks into turns, the pilot boat is a deep-V, the tug is a displacement hull that puts her power into pull, not speed. When Shipyard boats first capsized in turns, the cause was read off their own GZ curves (a centre of gravity 1.09 m above the waterline) and fixed with a lift-based roll stiffness about the apparent vertical.

The coast pipeline

Real coastlines from open data

The owner's brief: model every harbour the map data has, never just three. An offline tool chain in tools/coast/ reads OpenStreetMap, OpenSeaMap, Copernicus heights, ESA land cover and EMODnet depths, and writes a region as data: the game never calls a data server.

1
FetchOSM in 0.05° tiles, heights and land cover as cloud GeoTIFF windows, depths from EMODnet.
2
ReconcileA 10 m grid; the coastline seeds land and sea by flood fill; land takes the DEM, the sea a shelf blending into bathymetry.
3
MeshGreedy Delaunay to a triangle budget, error weighted by a viewshed from the sea: only 43 % of Oban's land is ever seen from the water, so hidden ground counts 0.03.
4
Harbours, houses, trees, boatsPiers and quays become the game's kit with berths every 16 m; buildings typed by tags or rules; a forest map; mooring fields laid where a harbour authority would.
5
Package and streamBig maps in 2 km chunks fetched within 1.5 km of any boat; a 50 m coarse grid is the ground everywhere else. Near chunks in full, far ones merged into one mesh.

Playing together

One host, no server, four boats

Peer-to-peer over WebRTC (PeerJS, 30 KB; its public broker only introduces peers). The host runs the simulation exactly as in single player. Players send tiny intents; the host sends snapshots twenty times a second, which crew render 0.12 s behind, interpolated, with a smoothed estimate of the host's clock so the waves match.

Because the world was plain JSON from the start, multiplayer never touched the physics. The hard parts were the real world: a 16 KB message limit that only bit once a career's world grew; GitHub Pages caching that put two players on different builds; a host who closes the tab (now every crew page elects the same successor with no messages). Then multi-boat: boats travel by token so any page builds the same type, your boat is the one you're aboard, far boats float on four stations every other tick, and two tugs can push one coaster.

Why it was easy to add

The simulation never touches Three.js or the DOM. Views read state and draw it. A control emits an absolute value as an intent and shows the authoritative state except while you hold it. So "multiplayer" was: send the intents somewhere else, and copy the state back.

An honest account

Where the human was essential

  • The eye. A roofline "way too narrow", hands that need "pitching forward 90°", a cruise ship whose "twenty decks" include five in the hull. Photos and sketches fixed in a round what words had missed.
  • The headset. The motion-comfort filter was measured to the degree and still reverted to 100 %: decoupling the view moved the hands off the controls. Only a VR test finds that. The sandbox has no headset, so every VR claim is "awaiting owner test".
  • The domain. "Flat out is fine, lugging on a tow overheats." "5:1 minimum, 7:1 recommended" for anchor scope. A tug's wheelhouse is forward. The owner's trade shaped the physics.
  • Saying no. Sketch-to-boat was built and removed at the owner's word; a pot hauler too. Milestones were skipped when they weren't worth it.
 

What the tool brought

  • Measuring before claiming. Headless screenshots, draw-call tables at four viewpoints, spectra of the engine against a recording, 40,000 deck points checked for walkability, golden runs to a millionth.
  • Flagging instead of building. "Bollard pull 4.2 t on 800 kW is low for a tug"; "the pilot boat at this waterline displaces 28.6 t and makes 12.2 kn where a real one carries twin 450 kW". Trade-offs stated, then the owner decides.
  • Diagnosis over assumption. "Hosting didn't work" reproduced against the real broker and traced to build caching, not hosting; a career that nobody could join traced to a 16,300-byte message limit.
  • Honest limits, every time. "Not checked: VR." "These streams could not be tested from the build machine." "Reported, not invented" where the map data is silent.

Try it

Play it, read it, build on it

  • Play: tartanski-cpu.github.io/SWVR. Desktop: WASD to walk, B to board, F to take the helm, W/S throttle, arrows steer, O for the menu. Quest 3: open the same address in the headset browser and press Enter VR.

  • Sail a real coast: Voyage, then Oban. Press J to fly if you want the view from this deck.
